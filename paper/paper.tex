\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) kills roughly nine in ten of the 137{,}000 Americans it strikes each year, and every minute without defibrillation lowers the chance of survival. Public automated external defibrillators (AEDs) can close that gap, but only if they are placed where arrests happen, \emph{and} can be reached when they happen. Using a public data set of 2{,}706 cardiac arrests in Virginia Beach, Virginia (2017--2019), a 52{,}708-node walking network, 929 candidate host sites with opening hours from OpenStreetMap, and the CDC Social Vulnerability Index, we formulate AED placement as a survival-maximizing facility-location problem, prove that its objective is monotone submodular (so a greedy algorithm is guaranteed $\ge 63\%$ of optimal), and solve it exactly as a mixed-integer program. In five-fold temporal cross-validation, our model achieves 21--64\% more out-of-sample expected survival gain than the standard maximal covering model for 25--200 AEDs, and the advantage holds in all 24 sensitivity scenarios. We find that planning as if AEDs were reachable 24/7 overstates their real benefit by 34--158\%, and that placing the same AEDs in outdoor 24/7 cabinets recovers 23--49\% more benefit. We also test, and reject, the hypothesis that AEDs should be weighted toward places where EMS was historically slow: location explains only 7\% of the variation in response time. Finally, a fairness constraint across vulnerability quartiles costs under 5\% of total benefit, and we prove a simple bound showing that, in Virginia Beach, full parity is unreachable with building-hosted AEDs alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Background \& Problem Definition}

\paragraph{Clinical urgency and defibrillation delays.} In the United States, emergency responders treated roughly 137{,}000 non-traumatic out-of-hospital cardiac arrests reported to the CARES registry in 2024, but only 10.5\% of those patients survived to leave the hospital alive \cite{cares2024}. For patients in ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia, survival hinges on delivering an electric countershock before irreversible ischemic injury takes hold. Every minute of delay causes a steep survival drop: in King County, Washington, predicted survival fell by 2.3 percentage points for every minute until CPR began, and by another 1.1 points for each minute of delay to defibrillation \cite{larsen1993}.

\paragraph{Bystander intervention and physical access barriers.} Automated external defibrillators (AEDs) allow lay bystanders to deliver early shocks guided by voice prompts. When a bystander applies an AED before EMS arrives, survival roughly doubles: 38\% versus 7\% across all treated arrests in the Resuscitation Outcomes Consortium \cite{weisfeldt2010}, and 66.5\% versus 43.0\% for observed public shockable arrests (adjusted odds ratio 2.62) \cite{pollack2018}. Yet bystanders attach an AED in only 12.6\% of public arrests nationwide \cite{cares2024}. In practice, bystanders face two basic physical barriers: the nearest unit is too far to walk to, or it sits behind locked doors in a building closed for the evening or weekend \cite{hansen2013,karlsson2019,deakin2018}.

\paragraph{Study setting: Virginia Beach.} Virginia Beach offers an unusual opportunity to study these spatial-temporal dynamics. Spanning roughly 460{,}000 residents across high-density coastal corridors, sprawling suburbs, and rural agricultural land, it is one of very few U.S. jurisdictions that publish complete, incident-level cardiac arrest records with the precise EMS response time for each dispatch \cite{custodio2022}. Across these records, the median time from the 911 call to the first unit's arrival is 7.2 minutes, and 41\% of patients waited longer than 8 minutes (Figure~\ref{fig:rt})---an interval where an accessible public AED could mean the difference between life and death.

\paragraph{Research questions.} Given a municipal budget of $p$ AEDs and a set of candidate host facilities, we examine four core questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] Does optimizing site selection for \emph{expected survival} over actual walking networks yield greater benefit on future, unseen arrests than standard models that maximize historical arrests covered within a fixed 100-meter buffer?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] How severely does the assumption of continuous 24/7 building access inflate projected survival gains, and what share of that lost benefit can outdoor 24/7 smart cabinets recover?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] Can historical ambulance delays guide prospective placement, or is dispatch latency too spatially variable to predict where AEDs will be needed next?
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] What is the price of fairness when requiring every social vulnerability quartile to receive an equitable share of expected survival, and does candidate site density impose a hard mathematical ceiling on parity?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}

\paragraph{Survival and time.} The link between time and survival is among the best-established facts in resuscitation science. Larsen et al.\ fit a linear model to 1{,}667 witnessed ventricular-fibrillation arrests and found that predicted survival falls from 67\% by 2.3, 1.1 and 2.1 percentage points for every minute of delay to CPR, defibrillation and advanced life support, respectively \cite{larsen1993}. Valenzuela et al.\ replaced this with a logistic model that depends only on the collapse-to-CPR and collapse-to-shock intervals and generalizes across U.S. cities \cite{valenzuela1997}. De Maio et al.\ fit a similar curve to EMS response time for all treated arrests in Ontario \cite{demaio2003}. Erkut, Ingolfsson and Erdo\u{g}an showed that replacing yes/no ``coverage'' with such survival functions changes, and improves, where ambulances should be based \cite{erkut2008}; we use their published forms of both curves.

\paragraph{Bystander AEDs work, but are rarely used.} Weisfeldt et al.\ found that an AED was applied before EMS arrival in only 2.1\% of 13{,}769 arrests, but that its use raised survival (adjusted OR 1.75) \cite{weisfeldt2010}. Pollack et al.\ found that the benefit of a bystander shock ``increased progressively as EMS response time became longer'' \cite{pollack2018}, and Hindborg et al.\ found the clearest survival benefit when EMS took 7--9 minutes \cite{hindborg2025}. These findings motivate RQ3.

\paragraph{Access in space and time.} Copenhagen data showed that 61.8\% of public arrests happen in the evening, at night or on weekends, while only 9.1\% of AEDs were accessible 24/7; limited hours cut coverage by 53.4\% during those periods \cite{hansen2013}. When the nearest AED was accessible, 30-day survival was 28.8\% versus 16.4\% when it was not \cite{karlsson2019}. In England, only 34.3\% of AEDs were available at night \cite{deakin2018}. Walking routes matter too: switching from straight-line to route distance cut 100-metre coverage from 24.2\% to 9.5\% in Copenhagen \cite{karlsson2021}.

\paragraph{Optimization models.} Toregas et al.\ introduced the location set-covering problem \cite{toregas1971} and Church and ReVelle the \emph{maximal covering location problem} (MCLP): place $p$ facilities to cover as much demand as possible within a fixed distance \cite{church1974}. A 2025 scoping review of 43 AED-placement studies found the MCLP to be the most common model \cite{petravic2025}. Chan et al.\ first applied optimization to AED siting in Toronto, using historical arrests and a 100-metre radius \cite{chan2013,chan2016}. Sun et al.\ added time-of-day availability and found that assuming 24/7 access overstated coverage by 21.5\% \cite{sun2016}; an \emph{in silico} trial in Copenhagen found optimized placement covered 24.2\% of arrests versus 15.8\% for guideline-based placement \cite{sun2020}. Siddiq et al.\ studied how the coverage radius changes results \cite{siddiq2013}. Pourghaderi et al.\ replaced coverage with a survival-maximizing objective in Singapore \cite{pourghaderi2021}, and Buter et al.\ used distance-decaying ``gradual'' coverage with greedy heuristics \cite{buter2024}. Other studies examined outdoor and landmark-based deployment in Paris \cite{dahan2016}, hotspot relocation in Switzerland \cite{aeby2021}, and cost-effectiveness in England \cite{brown2025}.

\paragraph{Equity.} Bystander CPR is far less likely in low-income Black neighborhoods (OR 0.49) \cite{sasson2012}, and both lay AED use and survival fall as the share of Black residents rises \cite{starks2017}. In England, arrests are concentrated in deprived areas while AEDs are concentrated in affluent ones \cite{brown2025}. Leung et al.\ showed that optimizing for coverage already shifted AEDs toward deprived Scottish neighborhoods \cite{leung2021}, but no AED study we found imposes an explicit fairness constraint or reports its cost. Bertsimas, Farias and Trichakis define the \emph{price of fairness}: the fraction of total benefit given up to achieve a fair allocation \cite{bertsimas2011}.

\paragraph{Gap.} To our knowledge, no prior study (i) tests whether an arrest's own observed EMS response time should weight AED demand, (ii) imposes and prices a fairness constraint by CDC Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) quartile \cite{flanagan2011}, or (iii) combines survival-based scoring, real walking routes and opening hours on a public U.S. city data set with temporal cross-validation.

\section{Proposed Solution / Theoretical Framework}

\subsection{From coverage to expected survival}
Let $I$ be the set of historical arrests and $J$ the candidate host sites. For arrest $i$ let $r_i$ be the EMS response time (minutes), $t_i$ the time of the 911 call, and $d_{ij}$ the walking distance from the arrest to site $j$. Let $a_j(t)\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether site $j$ is open at time $t$.

\paragraph{Timeline.} Measured in minutes from collapse, the 911 call is placed after a delay $c$. Without an AED, CPR begins when EMS arrives, at $c+r_i$, and the first shock follows $\delta$ minutes later. With an AED at distance $d_{ij}$, a bystander walks there and back at speed $v$ and needs $\sigma$ minutes to apply the pads, so the AED is ready at
\[
\tau_{ij} = c + \frac{2d_{ij}}{60\,v} + \sigma .
\]
Whichever comes first, AED or EMS, starts the treatment.

\paragraph{Survival gain.} Using Valenzuela's logistic model \cite{valenzuela1997}, as reproduced by \cite{erkut2008},
\[
S(T_{\mathrm{CPR}},T_{\mathrm{shock}}) = \bigl(1+e^{-0.260+0.106\,T_{\mathrm{CPR}}+0.139\,T_{\mathrm{shock}}}\bigr)^{-1},
\]
the gain in survival probability if arrest $i$ is served by site $j$ is
\[
g_{ij} = a_j(t_i)\Bigl[S\bigl(\min(\tau_{ij},c+r_i),\ \min(\tau_{ij},c+r_i+\delta)\bigr) - S(c+r_i,\ c+r_i+\delta)\Bigr]
\]
when $d_{ij}\le D_{\max}$, and $0$ otherwise. Thus $g_{ij}=0$ automatically if the site is closed, if it is too far, or if EMS would arrive first. Default parameters are listed in Table~\ref{tab:params}.

\subsection{The optimization problem}
Each arrest uses the best open AED nearby, so the expected survival gain of a set of sites $X\subseteq J$ is
\[
F(X) = \sum_{i\in I} \max_{j\in X} g_{ij} \qquad (\text{with } \max_{\emptyset}=0),
\]
and we solve $\max\{F(X): |X|\le p\}$. As a mixed-integer linear program, with $y_j=1$ if site $j$ gets an AED and $x_{ij}$ the share of arrest $i$ served by site $j$:
\[
\max \sum_{i,j} g_{ij}x_{ij}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \sum_j y_j\le p,\quad x_{ij}\le y_j,\quad \sum_j x_{ij}\le 1,\quad y_j\in\{0,1\},\ x_{ij}\in[0,1].
\]
For fixed $y$, the best $x$ puts all of arrest $i$ on its best open site, so the program computes exactly $\max F$. The classic MCLP is the special case $g_{ij}=1$ if $d_{ij}\le 100$\,m and $0$ otherwise, with every site assumed always open.

\subsection{A guarantee for the greedy algorithm}
\begin{definition}
A set function $F$ is \emph{monotone} if $F(A)\le F(B)$ whenever $A\subseteq B$, and \emph{submodular} if adding a site helps a small set at least as much as a large one: $F(A\cup\{k\})-F(A)\ \ge\ F(B\cup\{k\})-F(B)$ for all $A\subseteq B$ and $k\notin B$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
$F$ is monotone and submodular with $F(\emptyset)=0$. Hence the greedy algorithm, which repeatedly adds the site with the largest marginal gain, returns a set $X_G$ with $F(X_G)\ge\bigl(1-(1-\tfrac1p)^p\bigr)\,\mathrm{OPT}\ge(1-\tfrac1e)\,\mathrm{OPT}\approx 0.632\,\mathrm{OPT}$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Write $F=\sum_i f_i$ with $f_i(X)=\max_{j\in X} g_{ij}$, where all $g_{ij}\ge 0$. Monotonicity: enlarging $X$ can only raise a maximum. Submodularity: fix $A\subseteq B$ and $k\notin B$, and let $m_A=f_i(A)\le m_B=f_i(B)$. Adding $k$ changes $f_i$ by $\max(0,g_{ik}-m_A)$ for $A$ and by $\max(0,g_{ik}-m_B)$ for $B$. Since $m_A\le m_B$, the first is at least the second. A sum of monotone submodular functions is monotone submodular, so $F$ is too. The approximation ratio is the theorem of Nemhauser, Wolsey and Fisher \cite{nemhauser1978}. Note that opening hours enter only through $g_{ij}$ (which is $0$ when a site is closed), so the guarantee also holds for the hours-aware model.
\end{proof}

In practice, greedy matched the exact optimum at every budget we tested ($p=10$ to $200$), so the bound is conservative here. We use greedy's ordered site list in the interactive prototype (Section~\ref{sec:proto}), since it naturally answers ``which AED should the city buy next?''.

\subsection{Fairness and a feasibility bound}
Let $Q_1,\dots,Q_4$ be the arrests in each SVI quartile of census tracts ($Q_4$ most vulnerable). For the unconstrained optimum $X^\star$, let $T=F(X^\star)/|I|$ be its citywide average gain per arrest. A plan is \emph{$\varphi$-fair} if every quartile receives at least a fraction $\varphi$ of that average:
\[
\frac{1}{|Q_q|}\sum_{i\in Q_q}\max_{j\in X}g_{ij}\ \ge\ \varphi\,T\qquad\text{for } q=1,\dots,4.
\]
These are linear constraints in the MILP. The \emph{price of fairness} is $1-F(X_\varphi)/F(X^\star)$ \cite{bertsimas2011}. We use a fixed target $T$ rather than the plan's own average: otherwise, the solver could appear fairer by ``declining'' to use benefit in well-served areas, which is not how real AEDs work.

\begin{proposition}
Let $C_q=\frac{1}{|Q_q|}\sum_{i\in Q_q}\max_{j\in J}g_{ij}$ be the most any plan could give quartile $q$, with an AED at \emph{every} candidate site. Then a $\varphi$-fair plan exists only if $\varphi\le \min_q C_q/T$, no matter how many AEDs are available.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For any $X\subseteq J$, $\max_{j\in X} g_{ij}\le\max_{j\in J} g_{ij}$, so quartile $q$ receives at most $C_q$. Fairness requires this to be at least $\varphi T$.
\end{proof}
The bound is a simple statement, but it has a clear policy meaning: if a neighborhood simply has few buildings that can host an AED, no placement plan can make it equal, and other tools (outdoor street cabinets, drones, volunteer-responder apps) are needed.

\section{Experimental Design \& Implementation}

\subsection{Data}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cardiac arrests:} all 2{,}706 OHCA dispatches in Virginia Beach from 1 January 2017 to 30 June 2019, with call time, location (rounded to the block) and first-unit response time \cite{custodio2022}. 373 records list a response time of exactly 0 and one lists 128 minutes; we treat these as missing and impute them from a linear fit on road distance to the nearest of the city's 40 first-responder stations (fit on the 2{,}332 valid records; $r=0.21$). We repeat all results with these records removed.
\item \textbf{Candidate sites:} 929 public places from OpenStreetMap (Overpass API): places of worship (218), convenience stores (134), fuel stations (128), schools (120), banks (72), supermarkets (70), fitness and sports centres (77), fire and police stations (38), pharmacies (27), post offices (25), libraries (12) and others.
\item \textbf{Opening hours:} 209 sites have machine-readable OpenStreetMap hours; for the rest we assume typical hours by category (e.g.\ schools weekdays 7:00--16:00; places of worship Sunday morning and Wednesday evening; fire and police stations 24/7). We repeat the analysis with generous hours (every site open 6:00--22:00 daily).
\item \textbf{Walking distances:} shortest paths on the OpenStreetMap pedestrian network of Virginia Beach (52{,}708 intersections, 71{,}677 street segments) via OSMnx, up to 1 km.
\item \textbf{Vulnerability:} CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index 2022 \cite{flanagan2011} for the city's 112 census tracts (2022 TIGER boundaries; 111 have an SVI score), split into quartiles within the city.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental procedure}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Methods compared.} (a) random placement (average of 50 draws); (b) the classic MCLP \cite{church1974}: maximize arrests within 100 m walking, assuming every site is always open; (c) our survival-based, hours-aware model.
\item \textbf{Temporal cross-validation.} The 30 months are split into five half-year folds. For each fold, sites are chosen using the other four and scored on the held-out half-year, so every method is judged on arrests it never saw. Out-of-sample gains are added up over all 2{,}706 arrests.
\item \textbf{Metric.} Every method is scored by the same measure of reality: expected survival gain with real walking distances and real opening hours.
\item \textbf{Uncertainty.} 95\% confidence intervals from 2{,}000 bootstrap resamples of arrests; method differences are bootstrapped in pairs.
\item \textbf{Solver.} Exact MILP with HiGHS (via SciPy) and a lazy greedy algorithm; a negligible tie-breaker prefers sites with more historical arrests nearby.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Units: ``potential survivors''}
Because Valenzuela's curve describes witnessed shockable arrests, $F$ counts \emph{potential survivors}: extra survivors if every arrest were witnessed, shockable, and had a willing bystander. Nationally, 10.8\% of arrests are bystander-witnessed and shockable \cite{caresall}, so multiplying by 0.108 gives a rough real-world estimate; since not every bystander will fetch an AED, even this is an upper bound. We report potential survivors because they compare methods fairly; absolute lives are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Treating indoor host facilities as accessible around the clock introduces massive optimism bias into AED planning: across all tested budgets, assuming continuous 24/7 access overstates realized survival gains by 119--158\% (Table~\ref{tab:hours}, Figure~\ref{fig:hours}). Directly incorporating opening hours into the placement model delivers 49\% more expected benefit at $p=50$ than deploying under naive 24/7 assumptions, and installing those same 50 units in secure outdoor 24/7 cabinets recovers another 49\%. This temporal penalty is heavily concentrated during off-peak hours: between midnight and 3 a.m., an indoor-only deployment captures less than one-fourth the benefit of outdoor cabinets. Even when testing generous hypothetical operating schedules (every site open 6:00--22:00 daily), naive models still inflate projected benefit by 34--47\%, with outdoor cabinets recovering 23--28\%---substantially exceeding the 21.5\% overstatement documented in Toronto \cite{sun2016}.

\subsection{RQ3: steering AEDs toward slow-EMS areas does not help}
Our model was built to give more weight to arrests where EMS was slow. An ablation study (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) tests whether that ingredient matters, by replacing every arrest's response time with the citywide median.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule

\midrule
Classic MCLP (100 m, assumes 24/7) & 12.0 & 21.4 & 25.7\\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Ablation: out-of-sample potential survivors. The gain comes from combining survival scoring with opening hours; response-time weighting adds nothing.}
\label{tab:ablation}

Using each arrest's own response time does \emph{not} improve out-of-sample results; the version with a single median time is as good or better (at $p=100$ it is significantly better, difference 2.3, 95\% CI [0.4, 4.5]). A location-smoothed response time, built only from training data, does not help either. The reason is that EMS delay in Virginia Beach is barely predictable from location: a leave-one-out kernel average of nearby response times explains at most 7\% of the variance ($R^2=0.07$ at 1 km bandwidth). Delay depends far more on circumstances (which crew is free, traffic) than on place. Clinical studies show that the benefit of a bystander AED grows with EMS delay \cite{pollack2018,hindborg2025}, but our results suggest that, at least in this city, historical delays cannot tell a planner \emph{where} that delay will strike next.

What does help is the \emph{combination} of survival scoring with opening hours: each ingredient alone gives small or inconsistent gains, but together they produce the full improvement. Survival scoring values walks of 100--500 m that the classic model ignores, and opening hours make sure those longer walks lead to an AED that is actually reachable.

\subsection{RQ4: fairness is cheap, but full parity is impossible}
Without any constraint, the most vulnerable quartile ($Q_4$) already receives above-average benefit. The \emph{least} vulnerable quartile ($Q_1$) is the worst served (Figure~\ref{fig:fair}): these low-density suburbs have the slowest EMS (median 7.8 minutes versus 7.1--7.5 elsewhere) and the fewest candidate sites (0.9 per km$^2$ versus 3.0 in $Q_4$). In Virginia Beach, AED access is a geography problem more than an income problem, unlike in England \cite{brown2025}.

Requiring every quartile to receive at least 80\% of the citywide average costs only 1.5\% of total benefit at $p=50$ (3.0\% at $p=25$), and 85\% costs 3.5\% (4.9\% at $p=25$). But 90\% was infeasible at every budget, and for $p=100$ even 80\% was infeasible. This matches the Proposition: with an AED at every one of the 929 sites, $Q_1$ could receive at most $C_1=0.0142$ per arrest, while the $p=100$ plan averages $T=0.0188$, so $\varphi\le 0.0142/0.0188\approx 0.75$. Our solver found $\varphi=0.75$ feasible and $\varphi=0.80$ infeasible, exactly as the bound predicts.

\label{sec:sens}
We repeated the cross-validation under eight alternative assumptions: slower (1.0 m/s) and faster (2.0 m/s) bystanders, 300 m and 800 m walking limits, the De Maio survival curve, a 2-minute call delay, removing the 374 imputed response times, and removing the 20 most frequent addresses (which include healthcare facilities). In all 24 scenario-budget combinations, our model beat the classic MCLP, and the 95\% interval excluded zero in 20 of them (Figure~\ref{fig:sens}). The four exceptions are all at $p=50$, the budget where the advantage is smallest in the main analysis as well.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Policy guidelines for municipal AED deployment.}
Our empirical findings point to four concrete recommendations for Virginia Beach and comparable mid-sized cities.
First, incorporating building opening hours is the single highest-leverage operational lever available to planners. Deploying 50 AEDs in 24/7 outdoor street cabinets delivers greater expected clinical gain than placing 200 indoor units subject to commercial closing schedules (Table~\ref{tab:hours}: 59.7 versus 55.8 potential survivors).
Second, siting optimization should evaluate walking networks with continuous survival-decay scoring rather than arbitrary 100-meter straight-line buffers; retrieval walks of 200 to 500 meters still frequently reach patients ahead of arriving ambulances.
Third, municipal programs should avoid chasing historically slow EMS response zones. Dispatch latency in Virginia Beach is dominated by operational variance (such as crew availability and traffic) rather than persistent geographic deficits, leaving historical response times unable to guide future siting.
Finally, cities should adopt an equity floor across social vulnerability quartiles: mandating 85\% parity costs under 5\% of total citywide benefit. Because commercial host buildings are scarce in low-density suburban tracts, reaching full parity will require pairing indoor units with street-side cabinets, dispatched volunteer responders, or drone delivery.

\paragraph{Estimated clinical impact.}
Translating potential survival gains into projected lives saved requires adjusting for clinical arrest presentations in the field. CARES registry data indicate that roughly 10.8\% of non-traumatic OHCAs nationwide are bystander-witnessed with a shockable rhythm \cite{caresall}. Applying this benchmark, a deployment of 50 well-sited indoor AEDs corresponds to approximately 4.3 additional survivors across the 30-month study period (Table~\ref{tab:hours}; roughly 2.8 when evaluated out-of-sample across temporal folds, Table~\ref{tab:cv}). Housing those same 50 units in 24/7 outdoor cabinets raises this estimate to approximately 6.5 survivors. These figures represent practical upper bounds: even when a device is accessible, bystanders may hesitate, fail to recognize cardiac arrest, or lack retrieval assistance. Moreover, 71\% of cardiac arrests occur in private residences \cite{caresall}, beyond the reach of any fixed public AED. Public access defibrillation cannot replace baseline EMS capacity, but it serves as an indispensable multiplier during the critical early minutes of public shockable arrests.

\paragraph{Limitations and future directions.}
Several methodological constraints qualify our findings. First, the open dispatch data record 911 calls rather than confirmed cardiac arrests, omitting initial cardiac rhythms, witness status, and neurological outcomes; we therefore evaluated survival using validated logistic models from published literature \cite{valenzuela1997,demaio2003}. Second, incident addresses were rounded to the block level for privacy, and several frequent addresses represent healthcare facilities; sensitivity analyses excluding the top 20 addresses confirmed that model rankings remain unchanged. Third, opening hours for 720 of the 929 candidate sites were assigned by facility category; while sensitivity tests under extended hours confirmed the core findings, exact gains depend on empirical schedules. Fourth, 374 missing response times were imputed via road distance to the nearest station ($r=0.21$), though excluding these records produced identical conclusions. Fifth, our scoring assumes a willing bystander is present to retrieve the unit. In reality, bystander AED use remains rare \cite{cares2024}, scaling realized benefit down uniformly across all methods.

Future investigations should link municipal spatial data directly to clinical outcomes through the CARES registry, model time-varying bystander availability using mobile mobility data, and extend the integer-programming framework to co-optimize mixed portfolios of fixed indoor units, outdoor street cabinets, and autonomous drone delivery.

\section{Prototype: AED Placement Explorer}
\label{sec:proto}
To translate our optimization framework into an operational decision tool, we developed an interactive web map application (\emph{AED Placement Explorer}, a standalone HTML/JavaScript interface in the project repository). Planners can adjust a budget slider to add AEDs sequentially in greedy order ($p=1$ to $200$) and toggle dynamically between indoor sites with empirical opening hours, 24/7 outdoor cabinets, and the classic 100-meter MCLP. The map displays all 2{,}706 cardiac arrests colored by EMS response time alongside selected host facilities, annotating each site with its operating schedule and marginal survival contribution. A telemetry sidebar reports cumulative expected survival gain, percentage improvement over the classic covering baseline, and benefit distribution across social vulnerability quartiles. The complete analytical pipeline---including data preprocessing, walking network routing, mathematical optimization, cross-validation, and figure generation---is open source and fully reproducible from public records.

\section{Conclusion}
Using only open data, we showed that a survival-based, opening-hours-aware model places AEDs that save 21--64\% more expected lives on unseen arrests than the standard approach, and that ignoring opening hours more than doubles a plan's apparent benefit. We also found that two intuitive ideas do not hold in Virginia Beach: that AEDs should follow historically slow EMS, and that the most vulnerable neighborhoods are the least served. The broader lesson is that \emph{when} an AED can be reached matters as much as \emph{where} it is, and that some gaps in access cannot be closed by placement alone.
